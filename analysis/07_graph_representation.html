Recommendation

Research on how graphs solved problems elsewhere, measurements on the 610 solved items of the 20 M9 tests (556 of them with a CERMAT success rate), and a proposed design. It changes no data and no code.

There is no single best graph. The systems that lasted keep a few kinds of graph apart and link them. Google Maps keeps the road network, the live traffic and your route separate. Facebook stores one graph with a handful of typed nodes and typed edges. For CERMAT the same split works:

  • One problem. It is best shown as a small solution-flow graph per task. Its nodes are the given values and the intermediate results, an arrow means “computed from”, and each step names the one principle it applies. The item’s principle list is then derived from the steps instead of typed by hand. It is the only representation compared here that shows how an item is solved, and each step is evidence about one principle.
  • The whole exam. It is three linked structures: the bipartite graph of items and principles (the Q-matrix of psychometrics), a template of 16 slots (task 4 is algebra in 19 of 20 tests, tasks 9 and 10 are always constructions), and a coverage map painted on the principle graph.
  • The core. Keep the rebuilt principle graph as it is: the data supports its links. But its fine detail explains item difficulty no better than the 19 catalog tags, and about half of the difficulty comes from errors, format, position and dependence between items. So the graph needs an error layer and item attributes, not more principle nodes.

Order of work

  1. Pilot solution-flow graphs on about 30 items. Generate the computation items automatically from their expression trees with SymPy.
  2. Add misconception nodes, starting from the closed items where a wrong option is chosen more often than the key (33 of 110).
  3. Compute the slot template and the coverage map from data that already exists.

The student layer waits until a tool collects answers. No graph database is needed: Facebook’s own graph store, TAO, keeps its data in MySQL.

Lessons from other graphs

What runs through every case below: the structure that lasted was curated by people and had few types of node and edge; the numbers on it came from data; and users were shown a small, focused part of it.

One problem as a graph

The example is 2025 B 8: one figure, three items. A right trapezoid with bases 140 cm and 100 cm and height 30 cm is cut by one segment into a smaller trapezoid and a parallelogram with equal perimeters. Item 8.1 asks for the area, 8.2 for the perimeter, 8.3 for the perimeter of the parallelogram. CERMAT success: 8.1 38 %, 8.2 42 %, 8.3 only 5 %.

What the flow shows that the lists cannot:

  • Why 8.3 collapses to 5 %. It is the only item whose path needs a variable, and it rests on the slant side computed on the way to 8.2 (the blue arrow). A student who failed 8.2 has no 50 cm. The lists treat the two items as independent.
  • A step the annotation missed. “The cut equals the slant side, because opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal” is a real step. KON.VLAST.rovnobeznik covers that fact in its note, but 8.3 does not list it. Solving the task in graph form checks the graph, which is the test you set for it.
  • Where a hint goes. A tutor can ask for the 50 cm first, then for 2x + 100. Each answer is evidence about one principle, which removes the credit/blame problem of multi-principle items.

Other forms of one problem

  • P4, expression tree (automatic, for computations): SymPy parses an expression such as (66/30 − 55/30) · (−3) into a tree whose operators are the steps, and node types map to principles. That generates P3 for most computation items without hand work; 29 % of items have a POC or ALG main principle.
  • P5, relations of a figure (geometry, later): points, segments and relations such as AB ∥ CD, with theorems as rules, as Inter-GPS does. Useful for generating variants and drawing figures; costly; no diagnosis by itself.
  • P6, problem type (a tag on the task): „srovnání: o zlomek více / méně než“, „část a celek se zbytkem“, „dvě veličiny“. It groups items by the situation a student must recognise, which is what fails in the error items below.

The exam as a graph

  • Coverage map. The principle graph painted with how many tests use each principle and how many points depend on it, at zoom levels like a map. One test touches about 50 principles (41–58).
  • Items and principles. A bipartite graph, each item a hyperedge over its principles, tasks grouping items that share a figure. Every diagnosis model and every “similar problems” feature starts from it.
  • Slot template. CERMAT builds tests from slots; the template is slot → topics, weighted by how often each fills it.
  • Error layer. Misconception nodes on the principle they corrupt, with the wrong options that reveal them.
  • Plan graph. The prerequisite graph with durations and point values, a critical-path problem: what to learn next for the most points per hour.
  • Derived graphs. Co-occurrence, item similarity and communities, computed and never authored; for recommendations and for auditing.

What the data says

Which representation explains difficulty?

CERMAT’s success rate of each item was predicted from different representations of the item, by ridge regression trained on 19 tests and tested on the 20th, for each test in turn. R² is the share of the variance explained on the test the model did not see.

  • Format matters as much as content. Average success by format: A/N 60 %, OP 48 %, OV+OP 45 %, PR 44 %, MC 36 %, OV 26 %, constructions 25 %.
  • The prerequisite links carry information. Adding each item’s prerequisites improves the prediction (0.36 → 0.39): items that share prerequisites behave alike.
  • Finer is not more predictive. The value of the fine graph lies in explaining and diagnosing, not in predicting difficulty.
  • Length hardly matters. Items with one principle average 43 %, with six 30 %, and two to five lie between 33 and 37 %.
  • About half the variance is unexplained. The items furthest below prediction involve systematic errors (next table), integer boundaries (2026 B 8.1–8.2, 11–12 %), negative decimals (2022 D 2.2, 13 %), an unusual setup (2022 D 7.1–7.2, 4–10 %) and the last task (task 16 averages 16 %). None of that is fixed by another principle node.

Can the item bank tell gaps apart?

A gap in a principle can be isolated when an item needs it alone, or when two items differ by exactly that principle.

  • 36 of the 131 principles appear alone in some item.
  • 100 of 131 can be isolated this way using the explicit lists, but only 79 if items are represented by their lists plus all prerequisites. Keep the rule “list what the solution applies, not what it rests on”: it is the more diagnostic choice.
  • For the other 31, an app needs short diagnostic items of its own, or step answers from P3.

Other checks

  • Are prerequisites easier? In 67 of the 105 links that can be checked (64 %), the prerequisite’s items are easier, by 2.6 points on average. Weak support; item contexts differ, so the reversals are not a list of wrong links.
  • Co-occurrence is not prerequisite. 568 pairs of principles meet in some item; only 81 of them are prerequisite links, and 39 % of them cross areas against 18 % of prerequisite links. Tasks combine distant principles; prerequisites stay local. Communities found in the co-occurrence graph agree moderately with the 7 areas (adjusted mutual information 0.56). The constructions form a group of their own, and the word-problem principles join algebra.
  • Centrality. PageRank, with each principle’s use by items flowing down to its prerequisites, ranks POC.VYR.zaporna, ALG.VYR.scitani, POC.DEL.delitele and POC.ZLOM.kraceni highest. That is a better-founded priority than hand-set thresholds, but not a promise of points.
  • Items of one task are a unit. 185 of the 320 tasks have several items; success inside one task differs by 19 points on average and by up to 68.

Limits: aggregated success of items, not of students; every principle list is one solver’s judgement; C and D tests have a few hundred takers each; the 2024 tests follow a different design. These numbers show whether a representation agrees with how CERMAT items behave, not how students learn.

Recommended design

One typed graph in layers. The layers that exist stay as they are; two are added; the student layer comes later.

How it would be drawn

  • Zoom levels, like a map: areas and topics as groups in the overview, principles on zooming in, steps inside a task.
  • Focus first: one principle with its direct prerequisites and dependents. A whole-graph poster is what Khan Academy dropped.
  • A task as a route: the solution flow drawn as above, the answer at the end.
  • Lines for an overview: the longest chains drawn as metro lines through stations, as a second overview to try.

Decisions for you

  1. Steps. Should each task get a solution-flow graph, with the item lists derived from it? If yes, pilot about 30 items first: 2025 B 8, 2025 B 15, 2023 A 15, 2022 A 12, 2022 D 7, 2026 B 8, two constructions, three computation items generated from expression trees, and the seven items whose answers were taken from the key. Judge the pilot by insight: does the flow explain the item, and does it expose annotation errors like the one in 8.3?
  2. Errors. Should misconceptions become nodes, starting with the 33 closed items where a wrong option beats the key?
  3. Problem types. Should word-problem tasks carry a schema tag (srovnání, část a celek, změna, dvě veličiny)?
  4. Exam views. Should the slot template and the coverage map be computed in backend/core/ for the graph page, and later for mock tests?

None of this is needed before you review the rebuilt tree. All four additions keep the principle graph and its ids as they are.
